Example: Working with Data on Racialised Populations

DH Scholars Alexandra Ortolja-Baird and Julianne Nyhan describe archives as ‘haunted’ by the spectres of those whose voices have been excluded - intentionally or unintentionally - by conservation choices invariably underwritten by political and cultural contexts and biases. Every archive has a kind of negative image that remains unexamined and, correspondingly, uninterrogated. In their case study of collections of Hans Sloane—collections that notably seeded the modern British Museum—the ghosts are the non-western (primarily African) individuals who were the original creators and users of the culturally significant objects:“...the majority of individuals who either willingly or unwillingly contributed to the collection from beyond Europe were enslaved, coerced, or unremunerated for their efforts, and are irretrievable as Sloane remained silent about the original creators, users, and traders of his objects, despite recording the names of the western collectors who subsequently contributed these objects to Sloane’s cabinet”. When information relative to how locals used the objects was included, it was “treated dismissively as evidence of regressive or superstitious practices” and, crucially, “filtered and interpreted through western lenses”.

The gaps Ortolja-Baird and Nyhan describe are human-created, but they emphasise that subsequently using digital methods to explore such collections can re-inscribe and further exaggerate these gaps. When we look at a digital archive, what we might consider a complete resource in reality “represents many layers of appraisal decisions, many records that did not make the digitization cut, and, subsequently, an exponential multitude of silences”.

It is essential, in DH, to consider the impact of filtering through the lens of the digital. Computational methods require discrete encodings and thrive on consistent, quantified, and ample data. These conditions can in some cases influence data selection and transformation (e.g. simplifying categorization) that would seem to go against humanistic values.

Historian of Slavery, Jessica Marie Johnson describes how, in the context of her field, catering to the strengths of the digital can often mean choosing to use the documentation of slavers as a primary source, because they are more ample, complete, and strictly structured than any documents originating from the slaves themselves. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, for instance, was compiled from slave trader ship manifests and logs, and colonia trade and census documents. Johnson highlights how, despite the profound impact this resource had on scholarship, “immediately reshap[ing] debates about numbers of women and children exported from the continent, influencing work on women in the slave trade on the Afri can coast, slavery in African societies, and women in the slave trade to the Americas”, it failed to render black individuals as human; they “remained faceless, anonymous, disembodied”, and in “transfer[ing] the racial nomenclature of the time period (négre, moreno, quadroon) into the present and encode skin color, hair texture, height, weight, age, and gender in new digital forms”, such resources are in a way replicating the surveilling actions of slave owners and slave traders.”

Yielding to the demands of computational encoding can also sometimes create gaps from the get-go.

The 1890 American census was the first to use any kind of automatic tabulation, introducing Herman Hollerith’s tabulating machine — considered a direct ancestor of the modern computer. After being collected by surveyors, data was transferred to punch cards to be tabulated automatically. This new scheme required that all answers be discrete, or made to be so. Race was one of the characteristics encoded on the punchcards, and the 1890 census introduced several new options. The questionnaire was the first to include “Japanese,” (previously there had been no distinction between East-Asian countries) and “Indian” (first nations). Where, since 1850, only the categories “Black” and “Mulatto” (any person of mixed race) had been included, the rise of the eugenics movement drove congress to mandate the introduction of additional categories based on blood quantum. The instructions given to the enumerators explained that an individual having 3/4 or more “black blood” should be labelled as “Black,” 3/8-5/8 as “Mulatto,” 1/4 as “Quadroon” and 1/8 or less as “Octoroon”; any uncertainty or ambiguity that might have been noted by surveyors was lost in the transfer to punchcards.

The use of the tabulating machine meant that, even though the 1890 census received 25% more responses than the one in 1880, it was tabulated three and a half times faster. However, the way in which it changed data collection was not all positive; the fine grained African-American racial classifications introduced in 1890 were removed before the 1900 census, ”after census officials judged the data ‘of little value and misleading’”. However, even if later removed, the imposition and propagation of seemingly strict racial categories facilitated by the tabulating machine created data that would be the foundation of government policies that would continually re-inscribe them. Echoes of similar encodings continue to persist — independently of the knowledge they made negative. In their investigation of contemporary datasets used for image recognition, for instance, Scheuerman et al. found that the racial categories used in the Microsoft Celeb database were “Caucasian,” “Mongoloid,” and “Negroid”. They wonder at the contrast of the use of these terms, which are evidently sociohistorical, and the fact that, in modern practice, “classifications of race [...] are portrayed as insignificant, indisputable, and apolitical.”

Digital formats and methods demand a certain level of abstraction and formalisation. Though questions of the impacts of ontology and organisation are not foreign to many humanists, these choices are arguably less noticeable in a digital context, passed off as neutral requirements of the machine. Correspondingly, Digital Humanists must be even more attentive to the question of such choices.
