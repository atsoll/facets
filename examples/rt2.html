Example: Racist on Average - Sentiment Analysis and the Poetry of the Black Arts Movement

There is a history of using tools in knowledge production to extend the capabilities of humans, or even circumvent the ‘flaws’ of human senses; the microscope, for example, helped see smaller things than ever before, and photography now often replaces observation, being considered more accurate. With computers, what we’re augmenting or replacing is the brain. In the early days, this meant delegating tasks like mathematical calculations, so as to be able to complete them faster and at a larger scale. However, as the problems we apply computation to become more complex and less discreetly defined, it is important to carefully consider what work is delegated to machines.

n the context of his work on the poetry of the Black Arts Movement (BAM), Ethan Reed used computational Sentiment Analysis as a technique to explore the specificity of the genre. Reed chose rules-based tools, where classifications of sentiment are based on pre-established dictionaries of word-valence value pairs. More negative terms are associated with more negative values (closer to -1), and the inverse for the positive (closer to 1); overall sentiment of a text comes from combining them.

Reed knew going in that his chosen texts would present a challenge to automatic processing, as they were full of poetic features such as vernacular, irregular white space, and deliberate word misspellings that could not be normalised without betraying author voice. He correspondingly found some surprising results. The sentiment analysis judged what he perceived to be one of the most positive poems in the corpus—Come Sing a Song—as the single most negative text. He discovered that this came down to the model routinely classifying the word 'Black' as negative, despite the racial sense being designated as neutral in the model’s lexicon. Reed hypothesised—calculations appearing to confirm—that, failing to resolve the term's polysemy, the model simply averaged the valence values of the three possible senses of 'black', resulting in an overall negative. This course of action, though guessable because of the rule-based nature of the model, was completely black-boxed and not even featured in documentation. The classifier similarly struggled with terms whose sentiment was made opposite in the context of the poem (e.g. 'bad' in the line "sing a 'bad' freedom song").

Studying the classifier's struggles, Reed insists, contributed to a deeper consideration of the question of audience and, correspondingly, tone, which can be conceptualised in terms of an author's perception of their audience. A positive reading of Come Sing a Song would have relied on a specific interpretation of terms which is born of belonging to the community for which the poem was intended. This is something that the tool evidently lacked; its dictionaries had been derived from massive amounts of online product reviews, and therefore its ‘understanding’ of terms reflected dominant use.

In another example, one of the most terminologically violent poems in Reed’s corpus—The True Import of the Present Dialogue, Black vs. Negro.—was determined by the sentiment analysis tool to be relatively neutral; this occurred through a combination of the fact that the classifier simply marked vernacular terms for which it had no lexicon entry as neutral and the way in which 'negative' terms were often accompanied by 'positive' ones (e.g. "shoot straight"), the scores essentially cancelling out. The latter, though born of misunderstanding (in human terms), is an interesting computational reflection of the concept of "measured protest" which scholars had previously identified in BAM poems.

The value Reed finds in digital tools is in the way in which they "[do] not transform the text the way that a human reader does". The utility of computational tools in deepening his understanding lay in having an awareness of their inner workings and using them as an additional not an alternative lens for analysis, as part of a larger process of interpretation. If Reed had simply accepted the judgements of the machine as definitive conclusions without considering how it arrived at the interpretations, it would have been to the detriment of context and nuance—both of which are valued in humanistic inquiry.
