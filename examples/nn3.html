Example: Centering Errors

In the scientific tradition, error is generally understood as something that ought to be reduced as much as possible (e.g. through strict experimental protocols) and otherwise explained as a threat to the validity of results. In the humanities where there is more focus on contrasting interpretations, notions of correctness and, correspondingly, error, become a bit fuzzier.

In machine learning for example, the performance of a binary classification model is evaluated using a confusion matrix. When a model is trying to predict the label/category/etc. of an example, there can be one of four outcomes: True Positive (the model correctly identified an example), False Positive (the model misidentified an example as relevant), False Negative (the model failed to identify a relevant example) and True Negative (the example was not relevant and the model didn’t identify it)

A model is considered ‘good’ if it has higher precision and recall. Precision is the number of classification cases the model got correct divided by the total number of instances it identified as relevant (TP/(TP + FP)). Recall is the number of correct examples divided by the total number of relevant instances present in the data (TP/(TP + FN)). Improving precision and recall is premised on reducing the number of things that either the model incorrectly identified as of interest (FP) or failed to correctly identify as relevant (FN).

However, when it comes to using this kind of technology in the humanities, we might want to question if this is actually what makes a 'good’ or useful model. Literary historians Hoyt Long and Richard So, for example, used ML techniques to explore the evolution of the Haiku as a distinct poetic form. What they found is that the cases where their model identified something as a Haiku that was not labelled as such (FP) or didn’t identify what they thought of as a Haiku as such (FN) were the most interesting to analyse.

The math underpinning a basic classification algorithm means that more ‘problem’ cases emerge the more the classes (in this case Haiku/Not Haiku) “overlap or [...] exhibit internal variation”. The thing is, these are only considered ‘problems’ if the aim is to “assert a hard distinction between two classes”. However, “ if one is looking for points of overlap and confluence, as we are”, explain Long and So, “ if one is looking for points of overlap and confluence, as we are, then the problems are actually advantages. In fact, we would like to see more such problems”. They assert, therefore that “[w]hat the machine learning literature treats as misclassifications, then, we treat as opportunities for interpretation.”

Long & So were interested in computational ideas of error, but we should also think about computational handling of human error.

Humans are imperfect record keepers. Shakespeare and Company Project researchers Rebecca Sutten Koeser and Zoe Leblanc are very certain of this, given the state of the records they work with from the infamous Paris bookshop of the same name. The owner and primary record keeper often left unexplained gaps in the borrowing and membership records, and applied discounts and bent protocols for reasons that are opaque to modern researchers. Imputation is a common scientific statistical method for filling in missing information through, essentially, a mathematically informed best guess. The problem with using such methods in the humanities, explain Sutton and Koeser, is that they “assume consistent patterns” not present in messy humanities data. When they trialed imputation methods from the fields of finance and biology) on some of the Shakespeare and Company records, they found that the generated values seemed, in some cases, contextually plausible if highly uncertain. In other cases, however, where they tried testing the method by predicting known values, they found that the predicted data was highly inconsistent with the actual values.

Koeser and Sutton conclude that, though the level of uncertainty in these predictions means that they should not be treated as fact and integrated into further analysis, it is useful for Digital Humanists to investigate gaps, as they open up new questions and avenues of exploration. They propose a practice of ‘speculative reading’ that moves "the margins of our analyses to the center", framing "missingness as something that we can consider as an object of study outright".

In the case of both human and machine ‘errors’, these DH projects frame them not as problems, but opportunities.
